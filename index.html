Rut in New York and New Jersey

Pre-rut Oct 15–31 · Seeking Nov 1–7 · Chasing Nov 8–14 · Peak breeding Nov 15–22 · Post-rut Nov 23–Dec 5 · Second rut Dec 6–20
Peak breeding in the Northeast is mid-November (state fetal and fawning data put it around Nov 11–20). Dates shift a few days with the deer herd and the year.
What the research says
  • Time of day matters most. GPS-collared deer move mostly around sunrise and sunset. The best hours are usually the first two after first light and the last two before dark.
  • Time of year is next. Bucks moved about 20% more during the rut than after it, and they move in daylight most during seeking and chasing in early to mid November.
  • Colder than normal helps. Temperature was the only weather factor with a consistent effect: in fall and winter deer moved more on colder days. Unusually warm days slow daytime movement.
  • Strong wind and rain slow deer down. Movement drops as wind gets stronger, and deer move little during steady rain; they often move right after it stops.
  • Pressure and moon: little proof. Collar studies found no consistent effect of barometric pressure or moon phase, so the app gives them almost no weight. A cold front counts mainly for the cooler air behind it.
  • Hunting pressure. Heavy pressure, such as opening weekend of firearms season on public land, pushes deer to move more at night and in thick cover. Hunt escape routes and thick cover then.

Based on GPS-collar research (Webb et al. 2010), a Pennsylvania trail-camera study, and state rut data. Your own trail cameras and scouting beat any forecast.

Tracking tips
  • Red leaves, berries and some mushrooms look like blood to the camera too. Check every ring with your own eyes before you follow it.
  • At night use a bright white light. A red headlamp makes blood look dark and almost disappear.
  • Unsure if a spot is blood? A drop of hydrogen peroxide fizzes on blood.
  • Mark the last blood you found with flagging tape before searching ahead, so you can always go back to it.
  • Look on both sides of the trail and on brush and grass at the height of the deer's body, not only on the ground.
  • In low light hold the phone steady, or tap Capture, because a blurry frame hides small drops.

How to blood-track a whitetail

Most lost deer are lost in the first minutes after the shot: by moving too soon, or by not marking where the deer was hit and last seen. Follow these steps in order.

  1. Stay put and watch. Note how the deer reacted: a mule kick or hard run with a low head often means a heart or lung hit; a hunched, slow walk with the tail clamped often means a gut hit. Watch and listen until it's out of sight; a crash in the distance is a good sign.
  2. Mark two spots before they fade from memory. Where the deer stood when you shot, and the last place you saw it. Pick a tree or rock for each, take a compass bearing, and drop markers in Maps (type: Blood / recovery).
  3. Read the sign at the hit site. Quietly find your arrow or the first blood, without walking the trail. Use the table below to judge the hit, then wait the right amount of time.
  4. Wait. Pushing a wounded deer is the most common way to lose it: it will bed, then run farther if jumped. If in doubt, wait longer. Back out quietly if you need to.
  5. Track slowly, with one or two people. Mark the last blood with flagging tape or toilet paper every so often so you can always go back and see the direction of travel. Look ahead and on both sides, on leaves, grass and brush at the deer's body height, not just on the ground. Track marks and kicked-up leaves count too. Use Track in Maps to record your path.
  6. If the blood stops, go back to the last blood. Search in widening circles or a grid from there. Check thick cover, low ground, creek bottoms and water, and old trails leading away. Don't trample the last sign.
  7. If you jump the deer, stop. Note where it bedded, back out quietly, and give it several more hours.
  8. Approach a downed deer from behind, from the back and above if you can. Eyes open and glazed usually means it's dead; touch the eye with a long stick from behind to be sure. Be ready for a legal follow-up shot if needed.
  9. Tag it right away and report the harvest as your state requires before you move it far.

  • Hair color tells you where: long white hair often means a low hit (belly or brisket); dark brown, coarse hair means the back or upper side; short gray-brown hair is typical of the chest.
  • Warm weather or rain coming? Meat can spoil and rain washes blood away. You may need to start sooner on a likely-fatal hit, but go slowly and be ready to back out if the deer is still alive.
  • Is it blood? A drop of hydrogen peroxide fizzes and foams on blood. Use the camera view above to make blood stand out on red and brown leaves.
Tracking at night
  • Use a bright white light. Blood is hardest to see under red and green light, and some hunters find a warm-white or blood-tracking light helps.
  • Rules on carrying a bow or gun while tracking after legal hours differ; if you're unsure, leave it behind or call your state's conservation police first.
  • Tell someone where you are, bring a spare light, and record your path with Track in Maps so you can get back out.
Call a tracking dog

A trained, leashed tracking dog finds many deer that people can't, even the next day. New York requires the handler to hold a DEC leashed tracking dog license, and the handler must notify an Environmental Conservation Officer before tracking. New Jersey requires a Certified Deer Tracking Dog Permit. Call a licensed handler before you walk all over the trail, and don't track onto someone else's land without permission.

Camo check

Take a photo of yourself (or your camo) in the woods. Tap on the camo, then tap on the background right next to it. The score shows how far apart the two look to you and to a deer.

ΔE is the color difference between the two spots. Under about 10 they blend together, 10–25 is noticeable, over 25 stands out. Fine camo patterns also blur together for a deer, so they matter less than overall color and brightness.

Shine finder

Reflections from a watch, glasses, a scope lens, a phone screen or a shiny gun barrel flash bright to a deer even when your colors blend in. Photograph yourself in your gear, ideally with a flash or in sun, and the app rings the shiny spots.

  • Skip fabric softener and dryer sheets; they add scent.
  • Run the washer empty once first, because leftover regular detergent can put brighteners back on your gear.

  • Formulas change, so check the bottle says "no UV brighteners" when you buy it.
How to check your clothes for UV brighteners
Safety warning. UV light can damage your eyes and skin. Never look into the beam and never shine it at anyone's eyes, including your own, children's or pets'. Watch for reflections off mirrors, glass and shiny surfaces. Don't shine it on your skin, and keep your hands out of the beam while you check. Wear UV-blocking glasses (labeled UV400) if you use the light often, and keep it away from children.
  1. Use a flashlight sold as 365 nm. Cheaper 395 nm lights also give off a lot of visible violet light, which makes the glow hard to judge.
  2. Go into a fully dark room and give your eyes a minute or two to adjust.
  3. Test the light first on a sheet of white printer paper. Most printer paper contains brighteners and glows bright blue-white, so you know what a "positive" looks like.
  4. Hold the light about 30–50 cm (1–1.5 ft) from the fabric and sweep slowly over every piece: jacket, pants, base layers, hat, gloves, face mask, backpack and boots.
  5. Look closely at seams, stitching, labels, logos and zipper tapes. Thread and tags often glow even when the fabric doesn't.
  6. Anything that glows blue-white has brighteners. Rewash it a few times in one of the Good detergents, or treat it with a UV-blocking spray, then check again.
  7. Check before each season, and again after anything is washed in a regular machine or with someone else's laundry.
How deer see: what this app simulates
  • Two color cones instead of three. Deer cones peak near 455 nm (blue) and 537 nm (green). With no red-sensitive cone, reds and oranges lose their color and fall toward dull olive or gray. Blaze orange is bright to you and to other hunters, but not to deer.
  • Lower sharpness. Acuity is estimated at roughly 20/100, sharpest along a horizontal band at eye level (the visual streak) and blurrier above and below it. Set the horizon slider to where the skyline or the deer's eye level falls in your photo.
  • Light conditions. The Light menu puts the same light on both halves so you can compare. In dim light both you and the deer lose color and sharpness, but the deer side stays much brighter: deer eyes are packed with rods and have a reflective layer (tapetum) behind the retina. Fog adds a haze that is thickest near the horizon line. Start from a daytime photo; the brightness levels are an illustration of the trend, not measurements.
  • Headlamp. The beam lights a spot in the middle of the frame. Try it with Dark night or Full moon night. White and green light look bright to a deer, because green is close to the peak of its color cone and its night vision. Red light looks much dimmer to a deer than to you, because deer have no red-sensitive cone. UV light is nearly invisible to you, but a deer's eye lets UV through, so it likely sees the glow. Brighteners in your clothes would also light up under UV, which a camera photo can't show.

Limits. Phone cameras filter out ultraviolet, so the app can't show UV brighteners in washed clothing, which can glow to deer. It also can't show a deer's nearly 300° field of view or how strongly deer react to movement. The blur is calculated for a typical phone lens; use Zoom 3× to judge it, since a small screen hides fine detail anyway.
